Tagore-Springleaf

Ecological Significance

Unknown to most, Tagore-Springleaf occupies a critical area along the Khatib Nature Corridor identified in the Ecological Profiling Exercise (EPE), serving as a habitat and connectivity corridor toward the north by linking the Central Catchment Nature Reserve and Khatib Bongsu. Unlike other sites in this report, Tagore forest is currently a military training ground, and thus inaccessible to the public. Though little is known about Tagore, the nearby Thomson Nature Park was once part of the same continuous forest patch prior to Upper Thomson Road’s construction in the mid-1950s, and likely has a similar forest typology.

While the canopy of the Thomson area retains plantation-era species (such as rambutan, for which the area was once renowned) the understorey shows active recruitment of native forest plants, indicating the site’s regeneration from formerly cultivated kampung land. The area is also a stronghold for the globally Critically Endangered Raffles’ Banded Langur, which depends on this forest and adjacent parks for feeding and movement. Other sensitive species, including the locally vulnerable Cryptic Painted Frog, have also been recorded here. The value of this forest has also been highlighted in the Singapore Terrestrial Conservation Plan (2024) and Our Wild Spaces (2021).

The Face of the Forest

The Raffles’ Banded Langur has become an icon for our last remaining forests. forests. This large, leaf-eating monkey depends on large, well-connected forests with a rich diversity of native flora in order to survive. Its continued presence is a strong indicator of a healthy, functioning forest ecosystem. They have been observed crossing over into Tagore from Thomson Nature Park, with several individuals falling victim to roadkill. While their population has steadily grown over the years, their future remains tenuous, especially if new development further encroaches on their limited habitat.

Beyond the Langurs, Tagore-Springleaf are also possible refugia for other locally- endangered wildlife usually encountered in our Nature Reserves. For example, species like the Sunda Slow Loris are elusive and rely on relatively mature forest for survival. The presence of such species show that forest patches outside our Nature Reserves can still provide valuable habitat for native wildlife, allowing them to forage, shelter and traverse across the landscape comfortably.

Why is This Area Threatened?

The entirety of Tagore and Springleaf forests are currently zoned for residential development. Clearing it would not only result in the loss of one of our largest and most important remaining forest patches outside a nature reserve, but also weaken the last remaining ecological connection between Singapore’s central nature reserves and northern green spaces.

Our Hopes for The Site

Tagore-Springleaf could be retained as a Nature Park, following the precedent set by Windsor and Rifle Range Nature Parks, both of which have been conserved for their ecological value despite being zoned for other uses. Protecting the site would recognise its important role as a major stepping stone facilitating the movement of wildlife between larger forested habitats.

Planning efforts should prioritise alternative development sites in the surrounding area, such as Orchid Country Club, whose lease is expiring in 2030. In addition, wildlife connectivity across existing transport barriers should be strengthened through measures such as rope bridges and eco-link crossings to improve habitat permeability. Given its ecological significance and connectivity function, no development should take place within the Tagore-Springleaf site.
