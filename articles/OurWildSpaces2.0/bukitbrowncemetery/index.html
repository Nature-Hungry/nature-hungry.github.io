Bukit Brown Cemetery

Ecological Significance

The Bukit Brown Forest is primarily an abandoned-land secondary forest, with clear evidence of past human disturbance, indicated by the presence of banana, dumb cane, African Tulip, and other kampung-associated species. While some areas are densely vegetated, much of the canopy remains dominated by cultivated species.

There are also multiple freshwater streams that run through Bukit Brown and support small fish populations. In areas where the canopy is closed, there are observable signs of understorey recruitment, highlighting promising forest recovery. Bukit Brown has the potential to function as an ecological stepping stone which enhances connectivity to the Botanic Gardens and even further south toward Dempsey, though it only supports wildlife capable of flight.

Heritage Significance

Named after George Henry Brown, the site was originally intended to be developed as a coffee and nutmeg plantation, but the venture failed due to Singapore’s tropical climate. The land was later acquired by the Hokkien Ong clan, who planned to establish a self-sufficient village for poorer clan members. Instead, the area was designated solely as a burial ground and became known as Seh Ong Cemetery. A portion of the cemetery was then acquired to form Bukit Brown Municipal Cemetery. In its early years, Chinese families were hesitant to conduct burials due to small plot sizes and perceived disregard for feng shui principles. However, following improvements to plot layouts and several high-profile burials, the number of interments increased.

The cemetery is the resting place of many prominent Chinese pioneers, including Eu Kong, mother of Eu Tong Sen, as well as Lim Nee Soon and Chew Boon Lay - names that remain familiar today through the places named after them. Bukit Brown also contains the graves of numerous individuals connected to World War II, reflecting the cemetery’s proximity to sites associated with the final battles before the British surrender in 1942. Tombs themselves offer a window into Singapore’s multicultural past. Many are ornately decorated with stone lions, Sikh guards, cherubic angels, liberty bells and other motifs drawn from different cultural and religious traditions. The Adam Rd and Bukit Brown sites look markedly denuded of vegetation from aerial photos from the 1950s (above). It was after the cemetery’s official closure in 1973 that secondary forest growth reclaimed the site after the cessation of active landscaping and maintenance. The dense growth seen in parts of both sites are at most 70 years old, showing the incredible regeneration potential of these sites, and elsewhere.

Why is This Area Threatened?

Parts of Bukit Brown are zoned for housing. The construction of a shell station for the Thomson-East Coast Line station along Jalan Mashhor indicate that future developments are likely to occur here.

Our Hopes for the Site

More vegetation could be retained at the fringe of the golf course north of Bukit Brown. The west of Bukit Brown should also be retained to preserve connectivity in accordance with NParks’ Ecological Profiling Exercise (pending findings from any future Environmental Impact Assessments in the area). There could be assisted rehabilitation of the forest, with removal of introduced species and reforestation of native species. Finally, the streams in the site could be naturalised, and those with species of Conservation Significance retained.
