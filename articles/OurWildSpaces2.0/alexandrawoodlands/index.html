Alexandra Woodlands

Ecological Significance

Given its proximity to the Rail Corridor, Alexandra Woodlands acts as an ecological stepping stone for the overall north-south connectivity islandwide, providing some refuge past the Southern Ridges for wildlife on its way to Clementi Forest. A key characteristic of this site is the existence of a seasonal freshwater swamp habitat. This swamp completely dries out in hotter months and fills during the rainy season, explaining the lack of fish but rich aquatic insect fauna such as dragonflies.

Why is This Area Threatened?

Alexandra Woodland is currently zoned for residential use, with a proposed road that would bisect the site and cut across the Rail Corridor. Its clearance would mean losing not only a rare and fragile swamp forest habitat, but also a landscape of significant heritage value, shaped by a long history of military and later commercial use, as well as a place around which a community has grown.

Heritage Significance

Before WWII, the original swampland was converted into a military hospital and later an oil depot supplying the British Navy. During the critical Battle of Pasir Panjang, Japanese attacks caused oil from the site to flow towards Bukit Chandu and catch fire, isolating the C Company from the rest of the Malay Regiment. The depot remained in use after the war until the drums were removed in 1975. The depressions they left behind, together with the site’s natural hydrology, eventually allowed the swamp to return.

Currently, the site is accessible by a small hidden trail off the side of the Hang Jebat Mosque and is popular with trail hikers due to its more rugged nature. There is also a small deck built out over a fallen tree, sitting nearby the flooded freshwater swamp, with many tree tunnels and small clearings dotting the site. A small local community has grown around the site, cherished as a nature respite from city life.

Our Hopes for the Site

Alexandra Woodland could be conserved and designated as a Nature Park, with its historical significance and ecological recovery telling a compelling story of nature reclaiming a landscape shaped by decades of human use. The presence of freshwater swamp forest, one of Singapore’s rarest and most threatened forest ecosystems, further strengthens the case for its conservation. As the site is already relatively disturbed, carefully designed boardwalks could provide opportunities for recreation, education and appreciation of its natural and cultural heritage while minimising further disturbance. Rather than developing the woodland itself, future residential needs could be directed towards the site immediately to its north, which is already zoned for residential use and identified for detailed planning in the URA Master Plan.
