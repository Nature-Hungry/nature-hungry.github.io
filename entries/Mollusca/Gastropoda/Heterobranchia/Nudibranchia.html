Where seen?

Nudibranchs may be encountered on many of our shores. They are especially likely to be seen on reefs, on coral rubble and on rocky shores with sponges and other encrusting animals. Some are well camouflaged, while the colourful ones may also be hard to spot because they blend in with their equally colourful prey. Many are quite small.

What are nudibranchs?

Nudibranchs belong to the phylum Mollusca and are marine snails in the class Gastropoda that lose their shells as adults. The word ‘nudibranch’ means ‘naked gill’, and it refers to the feathery external gills that many species carry on their backs.

Nudibranchs have lost their shells as adults. To protect themselves, some produce distasteful substances, toxins and even acids. They advertise this with bright warning colours. Others are camouflaged to match their surroundings. Those that eat colourful creatures such as sponges or corals may themselves be colourful to match their prey. Being small and flat, they can easily hide in narrow spaces.

The Black-margined nudibranch is among the most commonly encountered nudibranchs on our reefs. St. John’s Island, May 05

The Lined chromodoris nudibranch is another commonly seen nudibranch. Pulau Semakau, Jun 05

The Bohol nudibranch is seasonally numerous. Sentosa, Jan 06

Some nudibranchs breathe with a flower-like feathery external gill on their backs. Others lack this feathery gill, and their gills are hidden between the body mantle and the foot. Many nudibranchs have two pairs of tentacles. One pair is near the mouth and is called oral tentacles, usually used to sense things by touch. The second pair is on top of the head and is called rhinophores. Rhinophores are believed to smell or detect chemicals used in the hunt for prey and mates.

Sometimes confused with flatworms, as well as other sea slugs that look similar but belong to different orders. Here is more on how to tell nudibranchs from flatworms and nudibranchs from other sea slugs.

Aeolid nudibranchs carry stinging cells from their prey in their cerata. Pasir Ris, Dec 08

The Blue dragon nudibranch can be numerous at times. Beting Bronok, Jul 03

The Melibe nudibranch eats tiny crustaceans, capturing them with an expandable hood. Tuas, Mar 09

Stored stolen stingers: Aeolid nudibranchs have long, slender bodies with clusters or rows of elongated structures called cerata. These contain extensions of the digestive system and may also help the nudibranch breathe. Some nudibranchs, like the Cerberilla nudibranch (Cerberilla sp.), protect themselves with the stingers of the sea anemones or corals they eat. These stingers are passed, undischarged, to the cerata. The cerata have special sacs at their tips that contain the stinging cells of their prey. These stingers remain live and ready to fire off if the nudibranch is attacked.

What do they eat?

Most nudibranchs are carnivores, and each species usually specialises in a particular victim. Being small and slow, they feed on immobile creatures such as barnacles, sponges, ascidians, hard corals, soft corals, sea anemones, zoanthids, peacock anemones, sea pens and even the eggs of other creatures, including other nudibranch eggs.

Slugs with teeth: Like other molluscs, many nudibranchs have a rough tongue called a radula. Each species has specially adapted teeth to deal with its prey. Those that eat sponges have many scythe-like teeth to scrape at the food. Some nudibranchs lack a radula and simply secrete digestive juices on their prey and suck up the softened, soupy result. Others that feed on corals have well-developed jaws to hold onto the coral polyp while they gouge out the flesh with hooked teeth. Those that feed on bryozoans or ascidians have a pair of large teeth to cut open their prey.

A Polka-dot nudibranch chomping on a blue sponge? South Cyrene, Feb 11

The Orange-spotted gymnodoris eats other nudibranchs and slugs. Pulau Ular, Apr 06

A pair of Polka-dot nudibranchs possibly mating. Pulau Semakau, Jul 08

Fierce slugs: Some nudibranchs, like the Orange-spotted gymnodoris, eat other slugs and nudibranchs. The Melibe nudibranch eats tiny crustaceans, capturing them with an expandable hood.

Nudibranch babies: Nudibranchs are simultaneous hermaphrodites, meaning each animal has both male and female reproductive organs at the same time. They practice internal fertilisation. As a result, each nudibranch has a complex system of tubes to avoid self-fertilisation, to introduce sperm while also receiving sperm from a partner, and for laying eggs.

They mate in pairs, lining up side-by-side, facing opposite directions, in order to exchange sperm. Then they go their separate ways and each lays its egg mass, usually on the prey it eats or on a nearby hard surface. Eggs are often ribbon-like and laid in a rosette. Here are photos of nudibranch egg ribbons. In most species, the eggs hatch into free-swimming larvae that have shells. Often, the larvae only undergo metamorphosis and settle down when they are near their particular prey! The juveniles then lose their shells and become adult nudibranchs.

Human uses

Nudibranchs do not do well in captivity and are thus not extensively collected for the aquarium trade. Moreover, some nudibranchs, such as phyllids, produce toxins that may kill their tank mates. However, they are part of the attraction for divers and other visitors to natural habitats.

Status and threats

None of our nudibranchs are listed among the endangered animals of Singapore. However, like other creatures of the intertidal zone, they are affected by human activities such as reclamation and pollution. Trampling by careless visitors and over-collection can also have an impact on local populations.
